# My Teaching Philosophy

I always loved teaching. It wasn't very clear to me why. But over the years I have gathered some clarity on the various aspects that are enjoyable to me. 

- As an introvert, it is a way to speak (and learn) about something that is interesting to me (e.g: physics, ecology) in a social setting, where the people listening / conversing with me are also interested in the same things that I am. I also always enjoyed brainstorming with peers, organizing reading groups, and in general creating and participating in intellectual safe-spaces where we can talk and ask questions freely.
- I really enjoy science communication, especially spoken communication where I get realtime feedback from the audience. I like following the verbal and bodily cues of the audience - the nods, the confused frowns, the smiles or laughter at my attempts at humor - and tailoring what I say to keep the audience engaged.
- I enjoy the philosophy of science just as much as science itself. I also think having the right mental models of the systems we study, and the process of the study (how we do science, how we deal with cognitive biases and our sometimes wrong models of the world) is extremely important to be a good student and a good scientist. Improving our mental models is a constant process of evolution as we gather more knowledge and experience with a subject.
- I *truly* believe every single human is inherently smart, creative, and capable. The environment and life's circumstances may not allow everyone to express this in the same way. And I enjoy the art of figuring out how to bring out the best in everyone. I deeply believe it is a good teacher's duty to explore ways to make every student feel engaged and enjoy the subject.

The last point needs more elaboration since I feel it is an underappreciated feature of being a good teacher. Tending to each and every student, in a way that can engage even the ones that are struggling the most, takes a lot of patience, creativity and exploration. This requires a teacher to push back on standard metrics (e.g: what volume of material, chapters in a textbook were covered in a semester). This requires taking on some risk on trying some innovative methods (maybe blending theory and lab work in non-standard ways). When I taught physics 101 to pre-medical students at Boston University, I used to blend theory and lab sessions (I was originally assigned to teach just the labs) since I realized a lot of the students haven't absorbed the theory well. I spent the first half an hour to an hour of every 3-hour lab sessions teaching the theory, answering questions, and explaining how it ties to the apparatus in front of them. I also used to incorporate a puzzle / brain-teaser related to the topic of that session (e.g: what happens to ice with bubbles / metal pieces as they melt in a glass of water?, or what happens to a spool when you pull the string from the bottom?). These immersive sessions led to a much deeper understanding of both the theory and experiments. I also consistently received the best feedback from my students.

I also enjoyed mentoring PhD students. Here I would spend long sessions redoing calculations of a paper, or tinkering with simulations, recognizing that even the more junior researchers are in reality my peers, and everyone has a valuable perspective on the problem. It was particularly rewarding to me to see students from non-quantitative backgrounds learn and start appreciating the math. It takes patience and empathy to make the intimidating mathematical concepts seem not only approachable to students from non-quantitative backgrounds, but enjoyable and leave them hungering for a deeper understanding.

Effective communication is part of being a successful in any field. Part of a good course is helping students become good communicators. This could be through student-presentations, group discussions, collaborative problem-solving / whiteboarding etc. From my experience facilitating a number of reading and discussion groups on a variety of topics, academic and non-academic, I have learnt ways of tinkering with the format of discussions, mediating debates on hot topics, and nudging to make sure everyone's voice is heard. Over the years, I have realized this takes practice, and keen perception to what animates people with different interests and personalities, and modulation of my role from an active to a passive facilitator.
